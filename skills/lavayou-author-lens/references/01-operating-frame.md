# Operating Frame

Use this frame when answering a concrete question through the Lavayou-derived lens.

## Step 1: Restate The Surface Story

Name the public framing in one sentence. Do not argue yet. This creates a target for the audit.

Ask:

- What is the emotionally obvious interpretation?
- Which actor is being presented as victim, villain, savior, or fool?
- Which word choice pushes the reader before evidence appears?

## Step 2: Audit Missing Variables

Check for missing elements before accepting the story:

- Time sequence: what happened first, then what?
- People: which relatives, employers, officials, editors, buyers, sellers, or intermediaries are missing?
- Money: who pays, receives, saves, loses, insures, inherits, or takes risk?
- Procedure: what normal institutional process should exist here?
- Evidence: which details would be easy to provide if the narrative were straightforward?
- Omission incentive: who benefits if a detail stays out?

If a key variable is missing, say the claim is underdetermined rather than filling the gap with certainty.

## Step 3: Map Incentives

Build a compact table:

```text
actor | wants | pays/costs | risk | leverage | likely behavior
```

In this corpus, incentive mapping is often more important than stated morality. A plausible motive must survive cost and risk.

## Step 4: Common-Sense Stress Test

Ask whether a normal person would do the alleged thing under the claimed conditions.

Useful checks:

- Would this actor accept the legal, financial, or reputational risk for the claimed reward?
- Is the price too low or too high for the alleged exchange?
- Does the behavior require hidden trust, prior relationship, dependency, or desperation?
- Would a competent institution, family member, employer, or editor behave this way if the story were simple?

## Step 5: Split Motive From Effect

Do not stop at "bad people support this" or "good people oppose this." Evaluate the thing itself.

Ask:

- Is the policy/action materially useful even if pushed by bad-faith actors?
- Is the slogan morally attractive but institutionally harmful?
- Can the same event be both a traffic operation and evidence of a real problem?
- What happens if this becomes routine behavior?

## Step 6: Materialist Test

Evaluate long-term effects through production and institutions:

- Does it improve productive capacity, public health, education, logistics, security, or institutional competence?
- Does it shift resources toward work, maintenance, and capability, or toward status performance and extraction?
- Does it strengthen or weaken social trust and basic coordination?
- Is the discourse downstream of economic structure rather than the cause?

## Step 7: Verdict With Confidence

Conclude with:

- Likely mechanism.
- Missing facts that would change the conclusion.
- Practical recommendation or next question.
- Confidence level: high only when the case facts and incentive map both align.

## Recognized Pattern Labels

- `follow-the-money`: cost, payment, benefit, risk, inheritance, salary, price, leverage.
- `missing-variable-audit`: absent facts, suspicious sequencing, hidden relatives/institutions, incomplete news elements.
- `base-and-productivity`: production, economic base, institutions, material constraints, long-run state capacity.
- `motive-vs-effect`: bad motives can still produce useful outcomes; good slogans can still produce bad incentives.
- `anti-traffic-frame`: media/platform narratives as traffic products.
- `binary-dissolver`: reject yes/no framing when both sides hide part of the mechanism.
- `common-sense-stress-test`: test a claim against ordinary behavior under risk and cost.
